# Sakhi — Round 1 Plan

## Idea

Sakhi is a voice-first agent for pregnant women in India. The woman talks. The agent does the work.

A pregnant woman sees a doctor only a few times. India recommends at least four check-ups. Many warning signs start between visits: a bad headache, a swollen face or feet, blurred vision, bleeding, less movement of the baby. By the next visit, she forgets much of it, and the doctor gets an incomplete history.

This affects every pregnant woman in India, in every state and language, in villages and cities, in every week of every pregnancy.

Her hospital registers her in the Sakhi app for Android or iOS. The app shows a calm, glowing orb. The orb is the agent, with a warm female voice in her own language. She never types and never uses menus. She just speaks: “Do din se sar dard hai aur pair sujhe hue hain.” (I have had a headache and swollen feet for two days.)

The orb asks one short question, such as “Dhundhla dikh raha hai?” (Is your vision blurred?) It knows how many weeks pregnant she is, so each question fits her stage. Using the Ministry of Health danger-sign rules, it then acts. If the signs are dangerous, it tells her to go to the hospital now and alerts her ASHA worker and a family member she chooses. If mild, it saves them and reminds her about visits and iron tablets. Before each visit, it reads her a short summary, and the doctor gets a one-page summary in her own words. The orb does not diagnose and does not advise on medicine.

Every answer must fit this woman, this week, this symptom. The orb answers only from what it knows about her: her weeks of pregnancy, her earlier symptoms, her hospital’s visit dates, and the Ministry of Health danger-sign rules. It never answers from general knowledge. Before it decides, it repeats back what it heard, in her language, and she confirms. If a symptom is unclear, it asks one more short question instead of guessing. If it is still unsure, it treats the case as more serious, not less, and sends her to her ASHA worker or hospital. Its replies are limited to four actions: alert, save, remind, and summarize. Doctors test it on realistic cases before launch, and every answer is logged so the hospital can check it.

Voice is the first and main way to use Sakhi. Only 10.6% of Indians speak English, and many women cannot read or type easily in any language. A form, a menu, or a chat window excludes them. Speaking is how women already describe their health to family and nurses. Without voice, the app has no users.

Privacy comes first. Her record stays with her hospital. We do not sell data or share it with private companies. Only government health professionals and public-health researchers see grouped data with no names, and only with her consent.

## Impact

Sakhi benefits the roughly 25 million women who give birth in India each year, and the ASHA workers and doctors who care for them. It matters most for women with the longest gaps in care. About one in three do not reach four check-ups (NFHS-6), which is roughly 9 million women a year. Only 55% take iron tablets for even 100 days. These women meet the health system a few times, and nobody hears them in between. India still loses about 22,500 women a year in pregnancy and childbirth (88 per 100,000 live births). Sakhi cannot stop all of these deaths. It aims at one thing: the delay between a warning sign and care.

Today, a woman with a bad headache, swollen feet or less baby movement waits, asks family, or keeps the worry until her next visit. The doctor then gets a rushed, half-remembered history, and the ASHA worker may not know at all. With Sakhi, she speaks to the orb the same day, in her own language. A danger sign brings a clear message to go to the hospital now, plus an alert to her ASHA worker and a family member. Smaller symptoms and missed iron tablets are saved and remembered for her. Before each visit, the doctor receives a dated one-page timeline in her own words. The wait changes from "until my next visit" to minutes. She no longer decides alone, and the doctor no longer starts from nothing.

We will track three signals, each with a target. Speed: 9 in 10 danger alerts get a human response within 2 hours, and 8 in 10 of those women reach a facility the same day. Habits: women using Sakhi take iron tablets and keep check-ups more often than similar women who do not, by at least 10 percentage points each. Safety: doctors review a monthly sample of conversations, and the target is no missed danger signs and fewer than 1 false alarm in 5. We measure each baseline first, so we can show the change and not only the final number.

The biggest risk is a missed or misheard danger sign. If Sakhi hears the wrong word, or stays calm when it should alert, a woman may feel safe when she is not. Hindi, Marathi and mixed speech differ by region, and we do not yet know the error rate on real voices. Sakhi does not diagnose. It repeats back what it heard, asks again when unsure, and treats any doubt as serious.
